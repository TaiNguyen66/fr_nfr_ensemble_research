\subsection{RQ1: Duplicate- and project-grouped evaluation}
\input{tables/rq1}

\textbf{S vs.\ G-dup.} Grouping the duplicate requirements left the estimates practically unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}). $\Delta$ ranged from $-0.003$ to $+0.005$, and the Holm-adjusted $p$ was 1.000 for all six models, so there is no clear difference for any model (0/6). Rankings changed only slightly (Kendall's $\tau = 0.733$, Table~\ref{tab:kendall}). PROMISE\_exp contains only 8 duplicate groups (16 of 969 rows), so this is a negative result for this dataset, not evidence that duplicates can never matter.

\textbf{S vs.\ G-proj.} Keeping each project in a single fold lowered macro-F1 clearly for all six models (6/6). G-proj scores were 0.661--0.755, compared with 0.851--0.893 under S. $\Delta$ ranged from $+0.134$ (M4) to $+0.190$ (M3), with Holm-adjusted $p \leq 0.007$. The variation between folds was also larger: the fold standard deviation was 0.05--0.11 under G-proj versus about 0.02--0.03 under S. The ranking of the models changed (Kendall's $\tau = 0.600$): M2 was best under S (0.893), whereas M4 was best under G-proj (0.755).

\textbf{Answer to RQ1:} \emph{Grouping duplicates gives no clear difference on PROMISE\_exp (0/6), whereas testing on unseen projects lowers macro-F1 by 0.134--0.190 for every model (6/6) and changes the model ranking.}

\subsection{RQ2: Oversampling before vs.\ inside the training fold}
\input{tables/rq2}

\textbf{T-bin.} On the binary task, oversampling before splitting gave higher estimates for every model and oversampler (Table~\ref{tab:rq2}): BEFORE reached 0.878--0.920 against 0.855--0.894 for IN-FOLD ($\Delta = +0.015$ to $+0.027$). None of the ten differences is clear after Holm adjustment ($p \geq 0.19$; 0/10).

\textbf{T-12.} On the 12-class task the inflation is large and clear for all ten comparisons (10/10). BEFORE reached 0.951--0.994, while IN-FOLD reached 0.495--0.597, a difference of $+0.392$ to $+0.491$ (Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$). The largest differences were observed for M3 ($+0.488$ with OS-R, $+0.491$ with OS-S).

\textbf{NONE vs.\ IN-FOLD.} Table~\ref{tab:none_infold} (descriptive) shows how much of the gap is due to oversampling itself. On T-12, correct in-fold oversampling raised macro-F1 for M1 ($+0.30$), M5 ($+0.17$ to $+0.18$) and M3 ($+0.11$ to $+0.12$), barely changed M2 ($+0.03$) and lowered M4 ($-0.04$). Without any leakage the 12-class task therefore lies at 0.292--0.563 (NONE) or 0.495--0.597 (IN-FOLD), far below the 0.951--0.994 obtained with BEFORE. On T-bin, IN-FOLD and NONE differ by $-0.001$ to $+0.004$ only.

\textbf{Answer to RQ2:} \emph{Oversampling before splitting inflates macro-F1 on the 12-class task by 0.392--0.491 for all models and both oversamplers (10/10), turning a task that reaches at most 0.597 into an apparent 0.951--0.994. On the binary task, the estimates are higher by 0.015--0.027, but there is no clear difference (0/10).}

\subsection{RQ3: Ensembles under leakage-aware evaluation}
\input{tables/rq3}

Under G-dup, both ensembles were clearly better than the random forest only (Table~\ref{tab:rq3}): M5 vs.\ M3 $\Delta = +0.032$ (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.003$) and M6 vs.\ M3 $\Delta = +0.042$ ($p < 0.001$). Against M1, M2 and M4 there is no clear difference (6/8 comparisons); for these, $\Delta$ ranged from $-0.006$ to $+0.017$, and all confidence intervals include zero. Under S the pattern is the same (2/8): M5 vs.\ M3 $+0.030$ and M6 vs.\ M3 $+0.040$. Hard voting therefore performs close to the better linear models.

\textbf{Answer to RQ3:} \emph{Under G-dup, hard voting and stacking are clearly better only than the random forest (M5 $+0.032$, M6 $+0.042$); there is no clear difference against logistic regression, the linear SVM or complement naive Bayes.}

\subsection{Replication on the Dalpiaz et al.\ dataset}
\input{tables/dalpiaz}

On the Dalpiaz data (T-bin), BEFORE again gave higher estimates than IN-FOLD for every model and oversampler, by $+0.034$ to $+0.081$ (BEFORE 0.775--0.822, IN-FOLD 0.716--0.754; Table~\ref{tab:dalpiaz}). As on PROMISE\_exp T-bin, none of these differences is clear after Holm adjustment ($p \geq 0.30$; 0/10). Testing on an unseen dataset (LODO) gave 0.405--0.511, compared with 0.709--0.746 under S, a drop of 0.21--0.31. This comparison is descriptive only because LODO has 4 folds. Rankings agreed little between S and LODO (Kendall's $\tau = 0.20$). In the no-both sensitivity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:noboth}), removing the 81 requirements labelled both functional and quality raised M2--M5 by $+0.035$ to $+0.074$ and lowered M1 by 0.103.

\subsection{Error analysis}

We inspected 30 misclassified requirements (T-bin, G-dup, seed 42): 15 from M2 and 15 from M5, each with 8 true-NFR and 7 true-FR rows. Ground-truth labels were not changed. The most frequent category was a \emph{functional verb in an NFR} (8 rows): quality requirements phrased as system actions, e.g.\ ``The system shall utilize HTTPS communication to ensure data confidentiality'' (labelled SE, predicted FR). Next came \emph{mixed FR/NFR} requirements (6), which state a function together with a quality, e.g.\ a list that ``must be color coded for easy identification''. Errors on true-FR rows were mostly caused by a \emph{quality keyword in an FR} (5), such as ``login'', ``available'' or ``time'', and by \emph{ambiguous or underspecified} statements (4). Four rows were flagged as a \emph{possible annotation issue}, e.g.\ an F-labelled requirement asking for a ``Windows XP look and feel''. The remaining rows were \emph{short or low-context} (2) or \emph{other} (1). Two of the 30 rows are the same sentence in one duplicate group, placed in the same fold by G-dup. Overall, most errors arise from requirements whose wording mixes functional and quality aspects, which a binary label cannot express.
