Two of them are large compared with the fold-to-fold variation: S vs.\ G-proj and BEFORE vs.\ IN-FOLD on the 12-class task. \subsection{RQ1: Duplicate- and project-grouped evaluation}
\input{tables/rq1}

\textbf{S vs.\ G-dup.} Grouping the duplicate requirements left the estimates practically unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}). $\Delta$ ranged from $-0.003$ to $+0.005$, below the 0.01 threshold for every model and far smaller than the fold standard deviation (0.018--0.031 under S, 0.018--0.025 under G-dup). Rankings changed only slightly (Kendall's $\tau = 0.733$, Table~\ref{tab:kendall}). PROMISE\_exp contains only 8 duplicate groups (16 of 969 rows), so this is a negative result for this dataset, not evidence that duplicates can never matter.

\textbf{S vs.\ G-proj.} Keeping each project in a single fold lowered macro-F1 for all six models. G-proj scores were 0.661--0.755, compared with 0.851--0.893 under S, a drop of 0.134 (M4) to 0.190 (M3). The variation between folds was also larger: the fold standard deviation was 0.052--0.113 under G-proj versus 0.018--0.031 under S. The ranking of the models changed (Kendall's $\tau = 0.600$): M2 was best under S (0.893), whereas M4 was best under G-proj (0.755).

\textbf{Answer to RQ1:} \emph{Grouping duplicates changes macro-F1 on PROMISE\_exp by less than 0.01 for every model, whereas testing on unseen projects lowers macro-F1 by 0.134--0.190 for every model and changes the model ranking.}

\subsection{RQ2: Oversampling before vs.\ inside the training fold}
\input{tables/rq2}

\textbf{T-bin.} On the binary task, oversampling before splitting gave higher estimates for every model and oversampler (Table~\ref{tab:rq2}): BEFORE reached 0.878--0.920 against 0.855--0.894 for IN-FOLD ($\Delta = +0.015$ to $+0.027$). These differences are consistent in direction but of the same order as the fold standard deviation (0.015--0.032).

\textbf{T-12.} On the 12-class task the inflation is large for all ten model--oversampler combinations. BEFORE reached 0.951--0.994, while IN-FOLD reached 0.495--0.597, a difference of $+0.392$ to $+0.491$. This difference is many times the fold standard deviation (0.002--0.006 under BEFORE, 0.040--0.075 under IN-FOLD). The largest differences were observed for M3 ($+0.488$ with OS-R, $+0.491$ with OS-S).

\textbf{NONE vs.\ IN-FOLD.} Table~\ref{tab:none_infold} (descriptive) shows how much of the gap is due to oversampling itself. On T-12, correct in-fold oversampling raised macro-F1 for M1 ($+0.30$), M5 ($+0.17$ to $+0.18$) and M3 ($+0.11$ to $+0.12$), barely changed M2 ($+0.03$) and lowered M4 ($-0.04$). Without any leakage the 12-class task therefore lies at 0.292--0.563 (NONE) or 0.495--0.597 (IN-FOLD), far below the 0.951--0.994 obtained with BEFORE. On T-bin, IN-FOLD and NONE differ by $-0.001$ to $+0.004$ only.

\textbf{Answer to RQ2:} \emph{Oversampling before splitting inflates macro-F1 on the 12-class task by 0.392--0.491 for all models and both oversamplers, turning a task that reaches at most 0.597 into an apparent 0.951--0.994. On the binary task, the estimates are higher by 0.015--0.027, a small difference of the same order as the variation between folds.}

\subsection{RQ3: Ensembles under leakage-aware evaluation}
\input{tables/rq3}

Under G-dup, both ensembles were clearly better than the random forest only (Table~\ref{tab:rq3}): M5 vs.\ M3 $\Delta = +0.032$ (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.003$) and M6 vs.\ M3 $\Delta = +0.042$ ($p < 0.001$). Against M1, M2 and M4 there is no clear difference (6/8 comparisons); for these, $\Delta$ ranged from $-0.006$ to $+0.017$, and all confidence intervals include zero. Under S the pattern is the same (2/8): M5 vs.\ M3 $+0.030$ and M6 vs.\ M3 $+0.040$. Hard voting therefore performs close to the better linear models.

\textbf{Answer to RQ3:} \emph{Under G-dup, hard voting and stacking are clearly better only than the random forest (M5 $+0.032$, M6 $+0.042$); there is no clear difference against logistic regression, the linear SVM or complement naive Bayes.}

\subsection{Replication on the Dalpiaz et al.\ dataset}
\input{tables/dalpiaz}

On the Dalpiaz data (T-bin), BEFORE again gave higher estimates than IN-FOLD for every model and oversampler, by $+0.034$ to $+0.081$ (BEFORE 0.775--0.822, IN-FOLD 0.716--0.754; Table~\ref{tab:dalpiaz}). These differences are again consistent in direction and comparable to the fold standard deviation (0.039--0.066). Testing on an unseen dataset (LODO) gave 0.405--0.511, compared with 0.709--0.746 under S, a drop of 0.21--0.31. Rankings agreed little between S and LODO (Kendall's $\tau = 0.20$). In the no-both sensitivity analysis (Table~\ref{tab:noboth}), removing the 81 requirements labelled both functional and quality raised M2--M5 by $+0.035$ to $+0.074$ and lowered M1 by 0.103.

\subsection{Error analysis}

We inspected 30 misclassified requirements from
PROMISE_exp (T-bin, G-dup, seed 42): 15 errors
from Linear SVM (M2) and 15 from Hard Voting (M5).
Each model contributed eight true-NFR and seven
true-FR instances. Ground-truth labels were
not changed.

Two authors assigned initial error categories
to the selected requirements and subsequently
reconciled their disagreements. Their initial
categorizations agreed on 15 of 30 instances
(50.0\%), yielding Cohen's $\kappa = 0.3911$.
Both authors used AI assistance during the
annotation workflow. Consequently, this
agreement should not be interpreted as fully
independent, unaided human inter-rater agreement.

After reconciliation, the most frequent category
was \emph{ambiguous or underspecified}
(8/30, 26.7\%), followed by \emph{mixed FR/NFR}
(7/30, 23.3\%) and \emph{functional verb in NFR}
(6/30, 20.0\%). An example of an ambiguous
requirement concerns using stored email addresses
as the primary means of communication.
A mixed requirement states that dispute cases
``must be color coded for easy identification''.
An NFR requiring the system to ``utilize HTTPS
communication to ensure data confidentiality''
was incorrectly predicted as FR.

The remaining categories were \emph{quality
keyword in FR} (4/30, 13.3\%), such as a
requirement concerning failed login alerts;
\emph{possible annotation issue} (2/30, 6.7\%);
\emph{other} (2/30, 6.7\%); and
\emph{short/low context} (1/30, 3.3\%).

Two selected errors, P0809 and P0752, contain
the same requirement wording apart from
punctuation and belong to one duplicate group,
which G-dup assigns to the same fold.

The three most frequent categories accounted
for 21 of the 30 selected errors (70.0\%).
The relatively modest initial agreement also
indicates that identifying a single primary
error category was challenging for the reviewers.
These findings are qualitative observations,
not verified causal explanations of model
behavior, and the observed proportions
cannot be generalized to the full dataset.
